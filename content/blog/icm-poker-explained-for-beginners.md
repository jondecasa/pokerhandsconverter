## What ICM is

**ICM** stands for **Independent Chip Model**. It converts the chips you hold in a tournament into an estimate of how much of the prize pool they're worth. It exists because chips are **not worth their face value**: doubling your stack doesn't double your prize.

## The basic idea

ICM assumes your chance of finishing first is your share of all the chips. It then works out your chance of second, third and so on, and multiplies each by the prize for that place. The sum is your equity in the prize pool.

## A worked example

Three players are left. The prize pool is $100, paid 50% / 30% / 20% (so $50, $30, $20). The stacks:

| Player | Chips | Share of chips |
|---|---|---|
| A | 5,000 | 50% |
| B | 3,000 | 30% |
| C | 2,000 | 20% |

Running the numbers (each player's chance of each finish, times the prize) gives:

| Player | Chip share | ICM equity (of $100) |
|---|---|---|
| A (chip leader) | 50% | about $38.39 |
| B | 30% | about $32.75 |
| C (short stack) | 20% | about $28.86 |

The chip leader holds **half the chips but only about 38% of the prize pool**. The short stack holds a fifth of the chips and about 29% of the prize pool. Chips are worth less as the stack grows, which is the whole point of ICM.

## Why this changes your decisions

- **Chips you lose cost more than chips you win are worth.** Doubling up gains less than busting loses, so you should avoid marginal gambles when a lot of money is at stake.
- **The bubble.** Close to the money, ICM pressure is highest. Short stacks are the ones the others want to avoid, so shoves tend to get folded to.
- **Pay jumps.** The bigger the gap between one place and the next, the more you should protect your spot.

## Where ICM doesn't apply

ICM matters when prizes are paid by finishing place, as in most tournaments, especially late. In a cash game chips are exactly worth their value, so ICM doesn't apply there. In the early stages of a big tournament the effect is small because there's little pay-jump pressure.

## A model, not the whole truth

ICM assumes all players are equally skilled and ignores position and future play. It's an approximation, but a very useful one. You can see the difference ICM makes when you track results: [tournament ROI](/blog/poker-tournament-roi-explained) is the long-run measure, and ICM is the tool for individual decisions.

## Bankroll note

ICM spots are where tournament variance comes from, so keep a bigger buffer than you would for cash games. [Bankroll management for online cash game players](/blog/bankroll-management-online-cash-games) is the foundation.
