## How tournament payouts work

In a tournament, the prize pool is built from the buy-ins (minus the fee) and paid to the players who finish highest. The **payout structure** says how many places are paid and how much each place gets.

- **Places paid.** In a typical multi-table tournament, only about the top 10 to 15 percent of the field is paid. In a small sit and go, it can be as few as the top 2 or 3.
- **Top-heavy vs flat.** A top-heavy structure gives the winner a large share and the lower places much less. A flat structure spreads the money more evenly.
- **The money bubble.** The bubble is the point where the next player to be eliminated misses the money. Players near it often play differently, because surviving is worth a lot.
- **Min-cash.** The smallest prize, paid to the last places in the money. It is often not much more than the buy-in.

A common three-place example is 50% / 30% / 20% of the pool. Large online tournaments are much more top-heavy.

## Why chips are not worth their face value

If prizes were shared by chips, a stack twice as big would be worth twice as much. They are not, because first place pays a fixed amount and there are also places below it. That mismatch is what [ICM, the Independent Chip Model](/blog/icm-poker-explained-for-beginners), measures: it converts your chips into your share of the prize pool.

## A bubble example

Four players are left and three get paid: $50, $30 and $20 from a $100 prize pool. The stacks are 4,000, 3,000, 2,000 and 1,000.

| Player | Chips | Share of chips | ICM equity (of $100) |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 4,000 | 40% | about $33.60 |
| B | 3,000 | 30% | about $29.49 |
| C | 2,000 | 20% | about $23.59 |
| D | 1,000 | 10% | about $13.32 |

The big stack has 40% of the chips but about 34% of the prize pool. The short stack has 10% of the chips yet about 13% of the prize pool. The short stack is worth more than its chip share because it can still win a prize, and the leader is worth less because extra chips are worth less and less.

## How the payout shape changes the pressure

Take three players with 5,000, 3,000 and 2,000 chips. With the same stacks, the chip leader's ICM equity changes with the structure:

| Payouts | Chip leader (50% of chips) |
|---|---|
| 70% / 20% / 10% (top-heavy) | about $43.39 of $100 |
| 50% / 30% / 20% | about $38.39 |
| 34% / 33% / 33% (flat) | about $33.50 |

With a flat structure, chips are worth much less than their share, so the pressure to avoid busting (and to avoid risky calls) is high. With a top-heavy structure, chips are worth more, so pushing for first place is more valuable.

## What this means for your decisions

- **Near the bubble, you can fold hands you would call in a cash game.** Losing all your chips costs more than winning the same amount gains.
- **Big stacks can apply pressure,** because medium stacks cannot afford to call.
- **Short stacks must pick spots,** since waiting costs them the fold equity they have now.

ICM is a model, not the whole truth. It ignores skill differences and position, so use it as a guide.
